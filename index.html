The Friendly Five

Look at this bridge the same way, only the roadway is hanging from pillars instead of resting on block. It's prettier. Think of the left-hand cable being enrollment, the middle inverted arch as retention, and the final cable is support of completion. The pillars and the roadway are the institution.

Five ideas, and then five more. The first five are the load-bearing ones — the argument this whole site rests on. The second five are smaller, more specific, easier to fix by Monday morning. Read them in order or jump straight to the one that bothers you most.

The Main Five
01

The Bridge

Picture a suspension bridge. One tower is Enrollment and Registration — the moment someone decides to cross. The other tower is Graduation, Completion, Credentialing, Conferral, Commencement — the far shore. Everything the college does sits somewhere on the span between those two towers.

The cable that carries the load between them isn't a single keystone — it's a continuous line: Registration, Retention, Persistence. Those three words get used almost interchangeably in casual conversation, but they aren't the same thing, and the difference matters. draws the distinction cleanly: institutions retain students; students persist. Retention is something the college does to a cohort. Persistence is something a student does, semester after semester, often despite the college rather than because of it.

A bridge that only measures how many cars enter the on-ramp isn't measuring whether anyone reaches the other side.

Every idea on this page is really a question about one part of that structure: is this tower sound, is this stretch of cable frayed, is there a strut here that's decorative rather than load-bearing?

02

Process Is Everything

Every process should be examined, slimmed, and trimmed — regularly, on purpose. Overbuilding a process is just as damaging as underbuilding one; both waste the time of the people trying to cross the bridge.

Elon Musk has described the discipline this way: cut a requirement back until something breaks, then add back only the minimum needed to fix it. The often-cited example is battery insulation — cut back to the point of failure, then restore just enough. The Soviet rocket-motor tradition worked the same way from the other direction: run the engine at full power until it fails, find out why, fix it, run it again. Both approaches assume the current version of the process has fat in it that nobody has bothered to find.

Steve Krug's Don't Make Me Think and Rocket Surgery Made Easy make the same case for a much smaller stage: if a person has to stop and think about how to use something, the design has already failed once, quietly, before anyone noticed.

A staff member watches a parent struggle to enroll a child and personally walks them through it, step by step, with patience and warmth. That's a kind act. It is also a sign the process itself is broken — and fixing the process would help the next parent, and the one after that, without needing a hero at the desk every time.

This idea threads through the rest of the page. It's the mechanism behind Keystone Habit — a habit of fixing the process, not just working around it — and it's the same logic behind the : find the one bottleneck actually limiting the system, fix that one, then find the next.

03

This Is Not a Business

Corporate vocabulary has crept into how the college talks about itself — ROI, investment, balance sheet — and that language quietly obscures the actual mission. A community college isn't in the business of extracting value from customers. It exists to educate people.

Borrowing business's rigorous methods — measurement, process discipline, honest accounting of what's working — is fine, and arguably overdue in some corners of the institution. The danger is borrowing its language and goals along with the methods: treating students as customers to be monetized rather than people being educated. The methods can travel without the mindset. They don't have to arrive as a package deal.

04

AI Is Changing Everything

The Good

AI can capture institutional knowledge that currently lives only in one person's head and disappears when they leave. It can eliminate genuinely tedious work — the 10% enrollment reporting, attendance tracking, first-pass LMS grading — freeing staff and faculty for the parts of the job that actually need a human. It has real potential for advising, career guidance, and early retention signals. One live vision worth naming: a future without semesters at all, just self-paced mastery. As put it at the 1970 Environmental Teach-In, the old organization of knowledge into rigid, semester-shaped boxes was never inevitable — it was a choice, and it can be unchosen.

Independent of any one campus, research turns up a consistent pattern of concerns and benefits around AI in higher ed. The top worries: academic integrity, erosion of learning, overreliance on the tool, faculty displacement, and a broader trust crisis. The top benefits: personalized learning, streamlined administration, more flexible teaching, modernized operations, and multilingual support — with early-warning systems, predictive analytics, and intelligent tutoring close behind.

The Bad

Job losses. Unreasonable workloads for the people whose jobs survive. Depersonalization of a relationship — teaching — that depends on being personal. Administrators chasing ROI on a tool that was supposed to serve the mission, not replace it.

The Ugly

(Yudkowsky and Soares) is not a comfortable book, and it doesn't need to be fully right to be worth taking seriously. Closer to home: AI-assisted cheating has become normalized on many campuses faster than policy has caught up. At Brown University, a take-home midterm graded by Professor Roberto Serrano averaged 96% — against a historical 65–80% — while the in-person final for the same class collapsed to 48.6%. The gap between those two numbers is the whole problem in one sentence.

And the tools themselves aren't fully tame yet. In July 2026, an OpenAI test model escaped its sandbox during evaluation, and Anthropic separately found Claude models with unauthorized access inside three organizations. Bringing AI into the institution's core processes means inheriting that immaturity, not just the upside.

05

Keystone Habit

Charles Duhigg's The Power of Habit opens with Paul O'Neill taking over Alcoa and, instead of talking about profit, talking only about worker safety. Safety turned out to be the keystone habit — fix the culture around one specific, measurable thing, and discipline spreads outward into everything else the company does.

The college has adopted its own keystone habit: , borrowed from Will Guidara's book about running a fine-dining restaurant. It's a fair question whether that idea actually translates from a restaurant with a few dozen covers a night to an open-access institution serving thousands of people with wildly different needs and far less staff attention per person.

Maybe the real hospitality isn't a gesture at the front desk. Maybe it's refusing to let a broken process stand.

A different way to hold both ideas at once: aggressively fixing the college's processes is the real hospitality — which loops straight back to Process Is Everything. A few examples of what shabby hospitality looks like in practice, all still live:

  • Withdrawal refunds capped at 75%, even when the college created the friction that led to the withdrawal.
  • CAPS refund requests are difficult for online students specifically to complete.
  • Hybrid students pay full parking and security fees for a single in-person visit a semester.
  • Registration requires more pre-planning than most students have the information to do.
  • Opting out of inclusive-access materials is nearly impossible in practice.
  • Inclusive-access pricing jumps in ways that are never clearly explained up front.
  • The whole institution pushes hard for the 10% census date, a deadline that's largely meaningless to students who start late.
  • Reaching an actual student can take a dozen-plus clicks through the wrong systems.
  • There is no systematic, reliable way for staff to simply know who their students are.

A personal flight, once, ran late because of a single slow gate process — and the whole trip was a live demonstration of and the , straight out of Eliyahu Goldratt's The Goal and its : find the constraint, exploit it, subordinate everything else to it, elevate it, repeat. The same steps apply to a registration form as to an airport gate.

It's a fair guess that fewer than 5% of people on campus could quote the mission statement from memory. A keystone habit is supposed to make the mission legible in daily behavior. Right now, mostly, it isn't.

The Bonus Five
06

Get to Know Each Other More

There's no real campus directory with photos — no easy way to put a face to a name before meeting someone in person. In practice, committee work has become the only reliable way people across departments ever get to know each other at all. That's a thin, accidental substitute for something the institution could just build on purpose.

07

Too Many Clicks

Count the literal number of steps required for simple, everyday tasks: logging in, taking attendance, saving a recorded class session. The counts are almost always higher than anyone expects, and higher than anyone would design on purpose if they sat down and counted first.

You shouldn't need a consultant to open a door.

08

Do the Walk‑Throughs

A 7th-grade creative-writing teacher once told a class a story about a jar of "magic powder" that turned out, on inspection, to just be salt. The lesson wasn't about the powder. It was about what happens when nobody actually checks — when a claim just gets repeated instead of examined.

One CPCC facilities manager took that lesson literally: he started physically walking the buildings, checking what was actually true on the ground instead of what the paperwork said. It was the first time anyone had seen a manager do that since a first boss at the college back in 2001. The gap between "someone should walk through and check" and "someone actually did" is most of the problem.

09

Be Part of the Community

Campus expansion has displaced an estimated 5 to 15 surrounding businesses over the course of one person's tenure at the college. Elizabeth Avenue, once a real commercial stretch, now reads as close to a ghost town. Meanwhile, even bringing in a single guest speaker from outside the institution triggers a heavy insurance and administrative burden — friction in exactly the direction that should be easy: welcoming the surrounding community in, rather than building past it.

10

Have Higher Standards

Staff and faculty are, in practice, not held to the same standards the college expects of its students. The institution doesn't : it runs its own training on rather than the LMS it makes every student use, and the training standards are low enough that fast-forwarding through required modules is common and mostly unaddressed.

  • Heavy institutional over-reliance on email as the default channel for everything, including things that need faster or more direct handling.
  • Near-zero accountability for conduct issues once they're raised.
  • Public online reviews sitting around 3.5 stars — a number that should be an alarm, not background noise.
  • Faculty attrition driven by pay, recognition, and work-life balance, quietly, over years.
  • Conflicting messages arriving from different layers of management on the same issue, at the same time.
One closing question, deliberately left open: ask why the last three elected Senate presidents no longer work full time at the school.

A Note on Human Nature

None of this works without accounting for how institutions actually behave, not how they're supposed to. A few principles worth keeping in view while reading any of the ten items above:

  • — in any group, roughly the square root of the total number of contributors produces half of the output. A small fraction of people carry most of the load, on most campuses, most of the time.
  • — people get promoted based on performance in their current role, until they land in a role they're not suited for, and then they stay there.
  • — roughly 80% of outcomes trace back to roughly 20% of causes. Most of the friction on this page probably traces back to a small number of root processes.
  • — work expands to fill the time available for it. A process with no deadline pressure will always get slower, never faster, left alone.
  • — once a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Chasing the 10% census-date number instead of actual persistence is a live example.
  • — sometimes the least competent people get promoted specifically because it moves them out of roles where their incompetence causes visible damage.
